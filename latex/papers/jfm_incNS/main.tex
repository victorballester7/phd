\begin{abstract}
	The effect of surface gaps on the stability and transition of an incompressible laminar boundary layer is investigated by \acl{DNS} and \acl{LSA}, for gap widths $w\in[0,130]$, depths $d\in[0,4]$ and Reynolds numbers $\Rey_\dstar\in[800,3000]$, based on the displacement thickness $\dstar$ at the gap. For gaps that are narrow or shallow, the long-time two-dimensional dynamics settle onto an equilibrium. In this regime the gap acts solely as an amplifier, adding an increment $\Delta n$ to the amplification factor $n$ of \acl{TS} waves; transition remains downstream of the gap, but its location is shifted upstream of the smooth-wall reference point. Increasing the gap size destabilises the equilibrium, and the order in which stability is lost depends on the depth. Shallow gaps become unstable to two-dimensional perturbations first, and the flow settles onto a limit cycle that appears as a self-sustained oscillation around the gap. Deep gaps become unstable to purely three-dimensional perturbations first, but this instability saturates without provoking breakdown. Transition occurs at the gap precisely where the two-dimensional limit cycle is itself unstable to three-dimensional perturbations, a criterion that reproduces the bypass boundary measured experimentally. The analysis shows that both two- and three-dimensional instabilities are a necessary and sufficient condition for transition to occur at the gap.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Aircraft surfaces that must sustain laminar flow are also manufactured surfaces. Panel joints, access doors and control-surface seams leave steps and gaps whose dimensions are set by manufacturing tolerances. Near a transport-aircraft wing's leading edge the displacement thickness is only $\dstar\sim O(0.1\,\mathrm{mm})$, so a millimetre gap spans several $\dstar$ and can move transition far upstream. Tolerances are expensive to tighten. The question is not whether gaps can be removed but how large one may be before it matters.

Under the quiet free stream of flight, a boundary layer follows the classical route to turbulence: \ac{TS} waves are excited by receptivity, amplify convectively and break down at finite amplitude. In practice this sequence is reduced to the $e^{n}$ method \citep{smithgamberoni,vanIngen1956}, which integrates the amplification factor $n(x)$ and declares transition where it reaches a threshold $N$ calibrated to the disturbance environment \citep{crouchNgKachanov2015}. Extending the method to an imperfect surface can be done in two ways.  Either it increases the amplification, by the increment $\Delta n=n_\text{gap}(x)-n_\text{flat}(x)$, over the smooth-wall curve at fixed $N$, or it lowers the threshold, by the drop $\Delta N$ in $n_\text{flat}$, between the transition locations measured without the imperfection and with it \citep{crouchKosoryginNg2006,perraudArnalKuehn2014,crouch2021predicting}. However, both methods rely on the same premise: the imperfection amplifies waves reaching it from upstream and creates none of its own.

Nothing guarantees that premise. Flight tests first identified a gap-width Reynolds number beyond which transition moves forward through \citep{nenniGluyas1966}. Laminar cavities were later sorted into open and closed regimes by whether the shear layer reattaches to the floor \citep{sinhaGuptaOberai1982,Zhang2005,Crook2013}. ONERA studies \citep{oliveBlanchard1982,seraudie2010,gentili2012} then varied the gap width $w$ and depth $d$ independently and mapped, in the $(w,d)$ plane, the threshold beyond which transition occurs at the gap, and \citet{beguetONERA} found the depth negligible on $\Delta N$ for $0.2<d/w<0.7$. For deep gaps, $d/w>5$, the compressible simulations of \citet{zahnRist} at $M=0.6$ instead found a non-monotonic dependence of $\Delta n$ on depth, which they attributed to an acoustic feedback whose resonance condition is set by the depth.

Only recently has the $(w,d)$ plane been swept systematically in a single facility. The experiments of \citet{jeffGaps}, at low Mach number ($M<0.1$) and low free-stream turbulence, span $0<w<110$ and $0<d<11$ and show that, wherever \ac{TS} waves still control transition, the data follow one of two limiting regimes selected by the aspect ratio. For $d/w\to 0$ the \ac{BFS} result $\Delta N=4.4d$ applies independently of $w$ while for $d/w\to\infty$, $\Delta N\approx0.1w$ applies independently of $d$. Beyond a boundary lying within $d\gtrsim1.8$ and $w\gtrsim19$, neither of these formulas holds: transition moves onto the gap itself and occurs at a markedly higher frequency, outside the \ac{TS} band. These points were labelled \emph{bypass} transition and excluded from the correlations.

This gap-located transition has been examined most directly by Medeiros and co-workers, all in compressible subsonic flow. Using global stability analysis at Mach numbers in the range $0.1\leq M\leq0.5$, \citet{mathiasMedeiros2019} attributed it to an instability of the shear layer spanning the gap, sustained by the edge-to-edge feedback loop of \citet{rossiter1964}. Extending the range to $0.1\leq M\leq0.9$, \citet{mathiasMedeiros2021} showed that the thickness of the incoming boundary layer selects which Rossiter mode dominates, whereas the Mach number controls the acoustic energy transfer that closes the feedback loop. Thus, compressibility was found to be strongly destabilising even at low $M$. Finally, at $M=0.5$, \citet{aguirreMedeiros2024} partitioned the $(w,d)$ plane into three regions: one in which all modes are stable, one in which only three-dimensional centrifugal modes are unstable, and one in which two-dimensional Rossiter modes are unstable as well. The boundary of this last region lies close to the bypass boundary of \citet{jeffGaps}. However, the nonlinear evolution of the three-dimensional modes, and how they interact with the two-dimensional ones, has received little attention.

Three questions are therefore left open. The first is where the amplifier description underlying both $\Delta n$ and $\Delta N$ ceases to apply, that is, what distinguishes a gap that merely amplifies waves arriving from upstream from one that sustains an instability of its own. The second concerns gaps that remain amplifiers: what mechanism sets the amplification, and why gaps of different sizes amplify differently. The third concerns gaps that sustain their own instability, where transition is bypassed and moves onto the gap. Linear instability marks where the steady flow ceases to exist, not where transition occurs, and a saturated unstable mode may or may not break down. It thus remains to establish what the unstable modes do once they saturate, at what stage the flow becomes three-dimensional, and whether the two- and three-dimensional instabilities act independently, compete or reinforce one another. This last question has no counterpart in the canonical cavity literature \citep{GharibRoshko1987,dynandcontrol,bresColonius2008,Citro2015}, in which the cavity sits in an otherwise stable stream rather than in a boundary layer that is itself convectively unstable.

We address these questions using \ac{DNS} and global and local stability analysis \citep{huerreMonkewitz1990,chomaz2005,theofilis2011} of incompressible flow over a spanwise-uniform rectangular gap, for $w\in[0,130]$, $d\in[0,4]$ and $\Rey\in[800,3000]$. Narrow and shallow gaps settle onto an equilibrium and act purely as amplifiers, adding an increment $\Delta n$. The local analysis shows that $\Delta n$ is set by a competition between two factors: the range of frequencies destabilised by the cavity shear layer, and the streamwise distance over which this destabilisation acts. Larger gaps destabilise the equilibrium, and the order in which stability is lost is set by the depth: shallow gaps lose stability to two-dimensional perturbations first and settle onto a limit cycle, whereas deep gaps lose it to purely three-dimensional perturbations, which saturate without breaking down. Transition occurs at the gap precisely where the two-dimensional limit cycle is itself unstable to three-dimensional perturbations, a criterion that reproduces the measured bypass boundary. Both instabilities are therefore necessary, and the onset of transition at the gap is set by a bifurcation of the flow rather than by an empirically-determined threshold.

The paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:numericalSetup} presents the governing equations and the numerical method. Section~\ref{sec:globalStability} computes the equilibrium base flows and their two- and three-dimensional linear stability. Section~\ref{sec:2Ddynamics} examines the two-dimensional dynamics: the amplification increment $\Delta n$ where the equilibrium is stable, and the limit cycles that emerge once the equilibrium loses stability. Section~\ref{sec:3Ddynamics} follows the nonlinear three-dimensional dynamics and tests the limit cycles for stability to spanwise-periodic perturbations, which yields the transition scenarios at the gap. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarises the results and discusses their implications.

\section{Numerical setup}\label{sec:numericalSetup}
The incompressible Navier--Stokes equations are solved using a spectral/\textit{hp}-element formulation. In nondimensional form, the governing equations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ns}
	\begin{aligned}
		\partial_t \boldsymbol{u} + \boldsymbol{u} \cdot \bnabla \boldsymbol{u} & = -\bnabla p + \frac{1}{\Rey} \Delta \boldsymbol{u}, \\
		\bnabla \cdot \boldsymbol{u}                                            & = 0,
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{u}={(u,v,w)}^\mathrm{T}$ denotes the dimensionless velocity field, $p$ is the dimensionless pressure, and $\Rey$ is the Reynolds number. Throughout the paper, $\boldsymbol{x}={(x,y,z)}^\mathrm{T}$ denotes the dimensionless spatial coordinates in three dimensions, with $x$, $y$ and $z$ denoting the streamwise, wall-normal and spanwise directions, respectively. The equations are nondimensionalised using the free-stream velocity $u_\infty$ as the characteristic velocity and the displacement thickness $\dstar$ as the characteristic length scale. The latter is evaluated at the upstream edge of the gap for the smooth-wall reference flow. Thus,
$
	\Rey \equiv \Rey_\dstar := u_\infty \dstar/\nu
$,
where $\nu$ is the kinematic viscosity of the flow. The Reynolds number is varied in the range $\Rey \in [800,3000]$. All the quantities in the paper are nondimensional unless otherwise stated.

All simulations are performed using the \texttt{Nektar++} solver \citep{nektar}, configured with a velocity-correction scheme and a continuous Galerkin projection \citep{vcs1,vcs2}. Time integration is performed using a second-order IMEX scheme, with the viscous terms treated implicitly and the advection term treated explicitly. Spectral vanishing viscosity is added to stabilise the numerical scheme in regions containing sharp gradients \citep{svv}.

The computational domain $\Omega$ is a rectangular cuboid, with a cavity of width $w$, depth $d$ and spanwise-uniform geometry attached to the lower wall to model the surface gap (figure~\ref{fig:domain}). The origin, $\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}$, is located at the upstream edge of the gap on the outermost spanwise plane. The domain dimensions are $h=150$, $\ell_{\mathrm{i}}=100$ and $\ell_{\mathrm{o}}=1000$, which are chosen to ensure a fully developed upstream boundary layer and to minimise the influence of the upper and downstream boundaries on the region of interest. The spanwise length is set to $b=4d$, which is sufficient to capture the relevant three-dimensional dynamics (see Section~\ref{sec:globalStability}). A spanwise-uniform Blasius profile ($\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_\mathrm{BL}(y)$) is prescribed at the inlet and no-slip conditions ($\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}$) are imposed on the solid walls. At the upper boundary, a far-field condition ($\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_\infty(x)$) is imposed. At the outlet, the outflow boundary condition of \citet{dong} is used to minimise numerical instabilities when energetic vortices leave the computational domain. Periodic boundary conditions are imposed in the spanwise direction.

The dimensions are anisotropically rescaled for clearer visualisation of the gap geometry.}\label{fig:domain}
\end{figure}
The domain is discretised using a Fourier expansion in the homogeneous spanwise direction, combined with a spectral/\textit{hp} element discretisation in each Fourier plane. Within a plane, a structured mesh of quadrilateral elements is used in the boundary-layer region and unstructured triangular elements in the far field. Between 4000 and 7000 elements are used in each simulation, depending on the gap geometry. On each element, a polynomial order of $p=5$ and $p=4$ is used to approximate the velocity and the pressure fields, respectively. This choice satisfies the inf-sup condition and prevents the numerical instabilities associated with an incompatible velocity-pressure discretisation \citep[see][]{infsupCondition,infsupTaylorHood}. The polynomial basis is constructed from modified hierarchical Legendre polynomials of the corresponding dimensions \citep{spectralhpSpencerBible}. In the spanwise direction the solution is expanded using 48 homogeneous Fourier modes, so that the full three-dimensional discretisation amounts to approximately $4\times10^{6}$ degrees of freedom. The classical 3/2 padding dealiasing rule is incorporated in the Fourier expansion and a spectral/\emph{hp} dealiasing technique \citep{mengaldo} is used in the spectral/\textit{hp} discretisation to prevent aliasing errors in the nonlinear terms. The resulting spatial resolution is sufficient to resolve the relevant flow features, as confirmed by grid-convergence studies.

For global \ac{LSA}, the Navier--Stokes equations~\eqref{eq:ns} are linearised around a base flow ${(\boldsymbol{U},P)}^{\mathrm{T}}$, so that $\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{U}+\boldsymbol{u}'$ and $p=P+p'$, and the resulting perturbation equations for ${(\boldsymbol{u}', p')}^{\mathrm{T}}$ are solved. The base flows are obtained from nonlinear simulations of~\eqref{eq:ns} and they can be either an equilibrium solution for classical \ac{LSA} or a periodic orbit for Floquet analysis. When the base flow is globally unstable, the selective frequency damping method \citep{sfd} is used to suppress the unstable modes and obtain the corresponding unstable equilibrium solution. When the base flow is a stable periodic orbit, the periodic orbit is extracted through naive time integration. The linearised equations are then solved to determine the corresponding eigenvalues and eigenmodes. These can be compactly written as% chktex 8
\begin{equation}\label{eq:linearizedNS}
	\begin{aligned}
		\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}}\partial_t\boldsymbol{q}'      & = \boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{U})\boldsymbol{q}',                                                                                                                                                   \\
		\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{\mathcal{M}} = \begin{bmatrix}
			                                       \boldsymbol{I} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix},\quad & \boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}(\boldsymbol{U}) = \begin{bmatrix}
			                                                                                                                                    -\boldsymbol{U} \cdot \bnabla - \boldsymbol{u}' \cdot \bnabla \boldsymbol{U} + \frac{1}{\Rey}\Delta & -\bnabla \\ \bnabla\cdot & 0
		                                                                                                                                    \end{bmatrix},
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{q}'={(\boldsymbol{u}',p')}^\mathrm{T}$, $\boldsymbol{u}'={(u',v',w')}^\mathrm{T}=\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{U}$ denotes the perturbation velocity field and $p'=p-P$ the perturbation pressure. For standard global \ac{LSA}, the base flow is constant in time. For Floquet analysis, the base flow depends on time and it is $T$-periodic, $\boldsymbol{U}(t+T)=\boldsymbol{U}(t)$. For this study, in both cases the base flow is independent of the spanwise coordinate, so perturbations separate into spanwise Fourier modes that evolve independently. Combining this with Floquet's theorem, solutions of~\eqref{eq:linearizedNS} are sought in the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:floquetansatz}
	\boldsymbol{u}'(x,y,z,t)
	= \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(x,y,t)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\beta z}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t},
	\qquad
	\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(x,y,t+T)=\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(x,y,t),
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ is the spanwise wavenumber ($\beta=0$ for two-dimensional perturbations) and $\lambda$ is the complex Floquet exponent. For a spanwise-periodic domain of length $b$, the admissible wavenumbers are $\{\beta_n = 2\pi n/b : n=0,\ldots,15\}$. When the base flow is steady, $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ is independent of time and~\eqref{eq:floquetansatz} reduces to the classical normal-mode ansatz. In both cases, the eigenvalue problem is solved with a matrix-free time-stepper approach \citep{barkleyBlackburnSherwin2008}. Integrating the linearised equations~\eqref{eq:linearizedNS} over a time interval $\tau$ defines the monodromy operator $\mathcal{A}(\tau)$, which maps an initial perturbation $\boldsymbol{q}'(0)$ onto $\boldsymbol{q}'(\tau)$. The eigenvalues $\mu$ of $\mathcal{A}(\tau)$ are related to the exponents by $\mu=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\tau}$, so that the eigenvalues of largest modulus correspond to the largest growth rates $\Re(\lambda)$. These leading eigenvalues and their eigenmodes are computed with an Arnoldi method that requires only repeated actions of $\mathcal{A}(\tau)$, that is, successive linearised simulations.

Local \ac{LSA} complements the global analysis and is used mainly to interpret the amplification of two-dimensional \ac{TS} waves across the gap. In this framework, the base flow is approximated as locally parallel at each streamwise location, $\boldsymbol{U}=(U(y),0)$, and perturbations are sought as normal modes of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:localansatz}
	\boldsymbol{u}'(x,y,z,t)
	=
	\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(y)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\alpha x + \beta z - \omega t)},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha =\alpha_r  + \mathrm{i}\alpha_i $ is the complex streamwise wavenumber, $\beta $ the spanwise wavenumber and $\omega =\omega_r +\mathrm{i}\omega_i $ the complex frequency. Since \ac{TS} waves are two-dimensional, only $\beta=0$ is considered, and substituting~\eqref{eq:localansatz} into the linearised equations gives the Orr--Sommerfeld problem \citep{schmid2001stability}% chktex 8
\begin{equation}\label{eq:OS}
	{\big({\mathrm{D}}^2-{\alpha}^2\big)}^2\hat{v}
	-\mathrm{i}\Rey\left[
		(\alpha U-\omega)\big({\mathrm{D}}^2-{\alpha}^2\big)
		-\alpha U''\right]\hat{v}=0,
\end{equation}
for the wall-normal velocity component $\hat{v}$, where $\mathrm{D}=\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}y$. The problem is subject to $\hat{v}(0)=\mathrm{D}\hat{v}(0)=0$ and $\hat{v},\mathrm{D}\hat{v}\to0$ as $y\to\infty$. Fixing $\Rey$, the pairs $(\alpha,\omega)$ for which~\eqref{eq:OS} admits a non-trivial solution form the zero set of a dispersion function $\mathcal{D}(\alpha,\omega;\Rey)$. The temporal and spatial formulations are two different real restrictions of this same zero set: the temporal one prescribes $\alpha=\alpha_r\in\mathbb{R}$ and solves for $\omega$, whereas the spatial one prescribes $\omega=\omega_r\in\mathbb{R}$ and solves for $\alpha$. In this work, only the spatial formulation has been used (see Section~\ref{sec:2Dstable}). Equation~\eqref{eq:OS} is discretised by Chebyshev collocation on $81$ Gauss--Lobatto points, with the domain truncated at $y_{\max}=45$. The points are clustered near the wall by the algebraic mapping of \citet{malik1990}. Following \citet{bridgesMorris1984}, the auxiliary variable $\hat{q}=({\mathrm{D}}^2-\alpha^2)\hat{v}$ is introduced to reduce it to a quadratic problem in $\alpha$, which is then linearised into a generalised eigenvalue problem and solved with the QZ algorithm. Each streamwise station and frequency is solved independently, and the \ac{TS} mode is selected from the full spectrum as the least damped eigenvalue whose phase speed lies in $0.15<\omega_r/\alpha_r<0.75$.

\section{Global stability analysis}\label{sec:globalStability}
\subsection{Base flows}
For sufficiently small gap depths and widths, the flow asymptotically approaches an equilibrium state (i.e.\ laminar steady base flow), provided that the initial condition lies within its basin of attraction. The equilibrium flow topology in the boundary layer outside the gap remains close to a Blasius profile, while the flow topology inside the cavity changes strongly with the cavity aspect ratio $w/d$ (figure~\ref{fig:baseflow}).

For small $w/d$ (figure~\ref{fig:baseflowA}), a recirculation vortex forms within the cavity beneath the shear layer spanning the gap. This topology is characteristic of an open cavity, for which the separated shear layer remains detached from the cavity floor and the cavity interior is dominated by recirculating flow \citep{sinhaGuptaOberai1982,Crook2013}.

Increasing the aspect ratio $w/d$ (figure~\ref{fig:baseflowB}) produces a more elongated, shallow recirculation region. The internal reverse flow is increasingly confined in the wall-normal direction, while the flow still features a recirculation vortex in the vicinity of the downstream edge of the gap.

Increasing $w/d$ further (figure~\ref{fig:baseflowC}), the cavity is very shallow and the recirculating motion is confined predominantly to a region adjacent to the upstream edge of the gap. In contrast to the deep-cavity case, the outer shear layer exhibits strong streamwise curvature. The resulting structure approaches the closed-cavity topology, for which the separated shear layer reattaches to the cavity floor before leaving the cavity \citep{Zhang2005}.

In the limit $w/d\to\infty$, the two cavity steps become asymptotically separated in the streamwise direction, and the flow may be interpreted locally as a \ac{BFS} flow in the vicinity of the upstream edge and a \ac{FFS} flow near the downstream edge. The shear layer separated from the upstream step extends across the gap and becomes increasingly less affected by the downstream edge. The associated recirculation remains confined near the upstream step. Near the downstream edge, the shear layer hits the wall and is deflected back into the external flow, resulting in a flow topology similar to that of \iac{FFS}.

\subsection{Stability of the base flows}
To assess the stability of the equilibrium states, a biglobal stability analysis is performed for both spanwise-uniform ($\beta=0$) and spanwise-varying ($\beta\neq0$) perturbations. The stability of base flows with respect to spanwise-uniform (two-dimensional) disturbances is characterised by the growth rate
\begin{equation}
	\sigma_{\mathrm{2D}}(w,d)
	=
	\max
	\big\{
	\Re(\lambda):
	\lambda\in
	\mathrm{spec}\big(
	\mathcal{L}_{w,d}^{\beta_0}
	\big)
	\big\},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{w,d}^{\beta_n}$ denotes the discretised linearised operator of~\eqref{eq:linearizedNS} for the gap configuration $(w,d)$ and spanwise wavenumber $\beta_n$, and $\mathrm{spec}(\cdot)$ denotes the spectrum of the operator. For spanwise-varying perturbations, the leading eigenvalue is computed for the range of spanwise wavenumbers $\beta_n$ described above, and the maximum growth rate over these wavenumbers is retained,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sigma3D}
	\sigma_{\mathrm{3D}}(w,d)
	=
	\max_{n=1,\ldots,15}
	\max
	\big\{
	\Re(\lambda):
	\lambda\in
	\mathrm{spec}\big(
		\mathcal{L}_{w,d}^{\beta_n}
	\big)
	\big\}.
\end{equation}
For every gap considered and $\Rey=1000$, the maximum in~\eqref{eq:sigma3D} is attained around $\beta_9$ or $\beta_{10}$, well inside the sampled range, and the growth rate at the largest wavenumber $\beta_{15}$ is small compared with this maximum. The set $\{\beta_n\}_{n=0}^{15}$ therefore contains the most unstable wavenumber and is sufficient to capture the leading three-dimensional instability.

The onset of instability is determined by identifying the values of $w$ and $d$ for which $\sigma_{\mathrm{2D}}$ and $\sigma_{\mathrm{3D}}$ become positive. This procedure defines the two-dimensional and purely three-dimensional stability boundaries in the $(w,d)$ plane. The frequency of the most unstable two-dimensional mode is given by
\begin{equation}
	\omega_{\mathrm{2D}}
	=
	\Im\left(\argmax\big\{\Re(\lambda) : \lambda \in\mathrm{spec}(\mathcal{L}_{w,d}^{\beta_0})\big\}\right),
\end{equation}
and analogously for the frequency $\omega_\mathrm{3D}$ of the three-dimensional modes. Figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000} compares the resulting stability boundaries with the experimental bypass transition boundary reported by \citet{jeffGaps} at $\Rey=1000$.

	\includegraphics{Images/3d2dStabilityCurveRe1000.pdf}
	\caption{Two-dimensional (solid green line) and three-dimensional (dash-dotted orange line) linear stability boundaries in the $(w,d)$ plane at $\Rey=1000$. Shaded bands indicate the interpolation uncertainty associated with the discrete simulation grid. The dashed brown line shows the experimental boundary beyond which transition occurs at the gap location rather than through the downstream primary instability mechanism \citep{jeffGaps}. The different regions indicate the corresponding dynamical regimes discussed in the text.}\label{fig:3d2dDynamics1000}

For deep gaps $(d \gtrsim 1.7)$, the purely three-dimensional stability boundary lies at smaller gap widths than the two-dimensional boundary. This indicates that spanwise disturbances can destabilise the flow before the equilibrium becomes unstable to two-dimensional disturbances. For shallower gaps $(1.4 \lesssim d \lesssim 1.6)$, the opposite behaviour is observed, with the two-dimensional instability occurring first. For deep gaps, the two-dimensional stability boundary closely follows the experimental boundary separating transition through downstream \ac{TS} waves from transition initiated at the gap location reported by \citet{jeffGaps}. For shallow gaps, however, the three-dimensional stability boundary provides a closer agreement with the experimental boundary.

To make this comparison precise, note that within the domain considered, $(w,d)\in[0,130]\times[0,4]$, each stability boundary has at most one critical depth for a given gap width. The two-dimensional and three-dimensional boundaries can therefore be written as functions $d=d_\text{2D}(w)$ and $d=d_\text{3D}(w)$, respectively. Truncation at the largest depth considered here, $d=4$, restricts their domains of definition to $[w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c},130]$ and $[w_\text{3D}^\mathrm{c},130]$, where $w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c}$ and $w_\text{3D}^\mathrm{c}$ are the widths at which each boundary attains that depth, $d_\text{2D}(w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c})=d_\text{3D}(w_\text{3D}^\mathrm{c})=4$. Since $w_\text{3D}^\mathrm{c}<w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c}$ (figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000}), only the three-dimensional boundary lies within the domain for $w\in[w_\text{3D}^\mathrm{c},w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c})$, whereas both do for $w\in[w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c},130]$. On the latter interval the two boundaries cross, and the experimental bypass transition boundary is closely described by their upper envelope,
\begin{equation}
	w\longmapsto\max\{d_\text{2D}(w),d_\text{3D}(w)\},\qquad w\in[w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c},130],
\end{equation}
so that transition is set by the two-dimensional boundary for the deeper, narrower gaps and by the three-dimensional one for the shallower, wider gaps. Global flow instability, on the other hand, occurs after crossing the lower envelope
\begin{equation}
	w\longmapsto
	\begin{cases}
		d_\text{3D}(w),                        & w\in[w_\text{3D}^\mathrm{c},w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c}), \\
		\min\{d_\text{2D}(w),d_\text{3D}(w)\}, & w\in[w_\text{2D}^\mathrm{c},130].
	\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The close agreement between the experimental boundary and the stability results suggests that both two- and three-dimensional instabilities play a role in bypassing the primary transition mechanism of the boundary layer (see Section~\ref{sec:3Ddynamics} for further discussion).

Together, the two envelopes partition the parameter space of figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000} into regions of distinct linear stability. Below the lower envelope lies region I, in which equilibria is stable to both types of perturbation; above the upper envelope lies region V, in which equilibria is unstable to both. Between the two envelopes only one of the boundaries has been crossed, and which one depends on the gap width. For the narrower gaps, where $d_\text{3D}<d_\text{2D}$, the three-dimensional boundary is crossed first and the intermediate region II consists of equilibria that are unstable to three-dimensional perturbations while remaining stable to two-dimensional ones. For the wider gaps, where $d_\text{2D}<d_\text{3D}$, the intermediate strip consists of equilibria that are unstable to two-dimensional perturbations while remaining stable to purely three-dimensional disturbances. This strip is split into regions III and IV, which share the same linear classification and are distinguished instead by their nonlinear dynamics, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:2Ddynamics}.

The two modes that define these boundaries differ markedly in spatial structure, in spatial extent and in time scale. The two-dimensional mode is uniform in the spanwise direction and corresponds to an oscillation of the cavity shear layer, whereas the purely three-dimensional mode has a non-zero spanwise wavenumber and takes the form of streamwise-elongated structures of alternating sign, reminiscent of the streaks observed in bypass transition of smooth-wall laminar boundary layers \citep{matsubaraAlfredsson2001,schmid2001stability}. Figure~\ref{fig:modes} compares the two for the gap configuration $(w,d)=(19,4)$ at $\Rey=1000$.

The two-dimensional and purely three-dimensional modes are localised differently. The two-dimensional mode stays close to the gap, with a comparatively uniform amplitude near its downstream edge, whereas the three-dimensional mode extends far downstream in the form of long streamwise streaks. These streaks are much weaker than the mode structure inside the cavity. The two modes are also well separated in frequency: here $\omega_{\mathrm{3D}}\approx0.0256$, which is about an order of magnitude below $\omega_{\mathrm{2D}}\approx0.2457$, so the three-dimensional structures evolve on a much slower time scale than the shear-layer oscillation. A frequency separation of comparable size between the three-dimensional mode and the two-dimensional shear-layer mode is also reported for open cavities \citep{bresColonius2008}.

Both modes can be identified with the two families reported for compressible gaps by \citet{mathiasMedeiros2019,mathiasMedeiros2021} and \citet{aguirreMedeiros2024}. The two-dimensional mode is an oscillation of the shear layer spanning the gap and corresponds to their Rossiter modes. In compressible flow, the Rossiter feedback loop is closed by acoustic waves propagating upstream from the downstream edge \citep{rossiter1964}. In the incompressible limit the pressure responds instantaneously to the impingement of the shear layer on that edge, so the feedback becomes purely hydrodynamic but the shear-layer oscillation persists. The three-dimensional mode, concentrated in the recirculating region inside the cavity, corresponds to their centrifugal modes. It arises from a centrifugal instability of the closed streamlines of the recirculating vortex. This mechanism has already been identified in incompressible open cavities by \citet{meseguerGarrido2014}, \citet{deVicente2014} and \citet{Citro2015}, and shown by \citet{bresColonius2008} to depend only weakly on the Mach number (for $M<0.6$). The same physical mechanisms therefore appear to underlie both the present incompressible modes and the compressible modes of Medeiros and co-workers. One difference is that the partition of \citet{aguirreMedeiros2024} contains no region in which only the two-dimensional mode is unstable, whereas such a region (III and IV) is found here for wide, shallow gaps.

\section{Two-dimensional dynamics}\label{sec:2Ddynamics}
Building on the correspondence between the two-dimensional stability boundary and the experimental transition boundary, the nonlinear two-dimensional dynamics of flow over gapped surfaces are examined. A systematic nonlinear \ac{DNS} parameter sweep is performed over gap depths $d$, gap widths $w$, and Reynolds numbers $\Rey$. The two-dimensional dynamical regimes identified at $\Rey=1000$ are summarised in figure~\ref{fig:2Ddynamics1000}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{Images/inc2dStabilityCurveRe1000.pdf}

Blue circles denote equilibrium states, yellow triangles denote limit cycles, and red stars denote chaotic states. The continuous green line represents the linear stability boundary separating the locally stable and unstable regimes, shaded in blue and red respectively. \end{figure}
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:globalStability}, for sufficiently small gap depths and widths the flow asymptotically approaches an equilibrium state. For deeper gaps, increasing the width takes the flow through a sequence of bifurcations that ends in chaotic dynamics. The first of these is a supercritical Hopf bifurcation (solid green line in figure~\ref{fig:2Ddynamics1000}), at which the equilibrium gives way to a stable periodic orbit. Widening the gap further eventually destroys the periodic orbit in favour of chaotic motion. The full route to chaos has not been mapped out here because turbulence studied in this paper is three dimensional. However, several period-doubling bifurcations have been identified along the way (Section~\ref{sec:bifurcations}), which is a classical signature of such a route.

The shape of the stability boundary itself is informative, as it is non-monotonic in the gap width. Widening the gap is initially destabilising: the critical depth decreases until it reaches a minimum near $(w,d)\approx(75,1.5)$, where the two steps interact most strongly. Beyond that width the trend reverses and widening becomes stabilising, as the critical depth increases as $w$ increases. As $w$ increases further, the coupling between the two steps weakens and the critical depth approaches the value set by two isolated steps.~\ac{DNS} at $\Rey=1000$, has shown that the onset of global instability is found at $d\in(2.75,3.0)$ for the \ac{FFS} and at $d\in(3,3.25)$ for the \ac{BFS} and very wide gaps are required to reach the asymptotic limit ($w\gg 250$).

\subsection{\ac{TS} waves amplification (2D stable regime)}\label{sec:2Dstable}
\ac{TS} waves are the dominant primary convective instability mechanism in two-dimensional laminar boundary layers. However, laminar boundary layers are globally stable to two-dimensional perturbations \citep{ehrensteinGallaire2005}. Therefore, a continuous source of noise is required to excite \ac{TS} waves. To evaluate and corroborate the \ac{TS}-wave amplification trends reported experimentally by \citet{jeffGaps}, linearised simulations are performed about the equilibrium base states $\boldsymbol{U}$ obtained from \ac{DNS} (blue circles in Figure~\ref{fig:2Ddynamics1000}; regions I and II in Figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000}). A body force $\boldsymbol{f}={(f_u,f_v)}^\mathrm{T}$ is added to the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:linearizedNS} to excite \ac{TS} waves. The forcing $\boldsymbol{f}$ has a divergence-free Gaussian spatial envelope and is driven in time by zero-mean, unit-variance Gaussian white noise, $X_t$:
\begin{align}
	f_u(\boldsymbol{x},t) & = -(y-y_{\text{c}}) \exp\big({-\alpha{|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{c}}|}^2}\big) X_t, \\
	f_v(\boldsymbol{x},t) & = (x-x_{\text{c}}) \exp\big({-\alpha{|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{c}}|}^2}\big) X_t,
\end{align}
where $\alpha = 100$, $\boldsymbol{x}_{\text{c}} ={(x_{\text{c}},y_{\text{c}})}^\mathrm{T} = {(-95, 0.5)}^\mathrm{T}$ and $|\cdot|$ denotes the Euclidean norm. The spatial envelope confines the forcing to a small circular region of radius approximately $0.5$ centred at $\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{c}}$. Although ambient disturbances in experiments are neither strictly white nor Gaussian, this stochastic model provides a standard and reproducible representation of the external forcing.

The linearised system is integrated forward in time until the initial transient has decayed and a statistically stationary response is reached. The amplification is then quantified in two steps: first a streamwise-dependent scalar disturbance amplitude $A(x)$ is extracted from the solution, and then that amplitude is converted into an $n$-factor.

The amplitude at a streamwise position $x$ is taken to be the wall-normal $L^2$ norm of the velocity perturbation,
\begin{equation}
	A(x) := {\||\boldsymbol{u}'|\|}_{L^2(\mathcal{Y}(x))} = {\left( \int_{\mathcal{Y}(x)} (u'^2 + v'^2) \mathrm{d}y\right)}^{1/2},
	\label{eq:amplitudeNorm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Y}(x)$ denotes the wall-normal section at $x$. Because the forcing is stochastic, the response fluctuates in time, and~\eqref{eq:amplitudeNorm} is replaced by
\begin{equation}
	A(x) := {\left( \int_{\mathcal{Y}(x)} {\left\langle u'^2 + v'^2\right\rangle}_\mathcal{T}\, \mathrm{d}y\right)}^{1/2},
	\label{eq:amplitudeTimeAverage}
\end{equation}
where ${\langle \cdot \rangle}_\mathcal{T}$ denotes temporal expectation over the stationary stochastic ensemble defined in the time interval $\mathcal{T}$.

Following the classical $e^n$ method \citep{smithgamberoni,vanIngen1956}, the growth of this amplitude relative to a reference position $x_0$ is measured by the local $n$-factor,
\begin{equation}
	n_{x_0}(x) = \log\left(\frac{A(x)}{A(x_0)} \right).
\end{equation}
The reference position $x_0$ is taken to be the location of minimum disturbance amplitude upstream of the gap. For brevity, the subscript is omitted hereafter, $n\equiv n_{x_0}$.

The amplification measured in this way can be compared with the prediction of local stability theory which, unlike the simulations, resolves the contribution of each individual frequency. Local \ac{LSA} is therefore performed on the same equilibrium base flows at discrete streamwise positions, excluding the immediate vicinity of the gap corners, where the parallel-flow approximation is not valid. Because the forcing is white in time, the boundary layer is excited over a band $\mathcal{F}$ of real frequencies. Thus, the spatial formulation of the Orr--Sommerfeld problem is the relevant one: at each position $x$ the wall-normal section $U(y;x)$ of the base flow is considered and~\eqref{eq:OS} is solved for a prescribed real frequency $\omega_r\in\mathcal{F}$. The least stable mode $\alpha(x;\omega_r)$ and its associated eigenvector $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(y;x,\omega_r)$ are retained. The eigenvector is taken such that its $L^2$ norm over the wall-normal section $\mathcal{Y}(x)$ is one. % chktex 8

Since the base flow varies in $x$, the wavenumber does too, and the strictly parallel ansatz~\eqref{eq:localansatz} must be replaced by its slowly varying counterpart. The perturbation is then written, in the manner of a WKBJ (Wentzel--Kramers--Brillouin--Jeffreys) approximation, as the superposition
\begin{equation}
	\boldsymbol{u}'(x,y,t)
	=
	\int_{\mathcal{F}}\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}(y;x,\omega_r)
	\mathrm{e}^{n(x;\omega_r)}\exp\left(\mathrm{i}\int_{x_0}^{x}\alpha_r(\xi;\omega_r)\mathrm{d}\xi\right)
	\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_r t}\mathrm{d}\omega_r,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
	n(x;\omega_r) := \int_{x_0}^{x} -\alpha_i(\xi;\omega_r)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,
\end{equation}
so that $-\alpha_i$ is the local spatial growth rate and $n(x;\omega_r)$ the $n$-factor accumulated by that frequency alone between $x_0$ and $x$. Taking $\mathcal{T}=(0,\infty)$, the time average amplitude in~\eqref{eq:amplitudeTimeAverage} reduces to
\begin{equation}	A^2(x) = \int_{\mathcal{F}} \mathrm{e}^{2 n(x;\omega_r)}\mathrm{d}\omega_r,
\end{equation}
which yields the local version of the $n$-factor,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:localnfactor}
	n(x) = \frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\int_{\mathcal{F}} \mathrm{e}^{2 n(x;\omega_r)}\,\mathrm{d}\omega_r\right),
\end{equation}
where $|\mathcal{F}|$ is the measure of the frequency band. For the configurations considered, $\mathcal{F}=[0,600]$, which is wider than the broadest unstable band of frequencies (see figure~\ref{fig:neutralcurves}), so that the integral in~\eqref{eq:localnfactor} is dominated by unstable modes.

The agreement is particularly good up to the peak of the $n$-factor curve. However, the local model does overestimate the amplification downstream. Several features of the local \ac{LSA} help explain the observed behaviour of $n$ in figure~\ref{fig:nfactorCurves}. Within the cavity, the unstable frequency range becomes broader. Since the system is forced with white noise, a wider range of frequencies is excited, resulting in a faster growth of the perturbations. This leads to the enhanced amplification observed in the $n$-factor curves.

Downstream of small gaps, such as $w=10$ and $w=15$ in figure~\ref{fig:neutralcurvesA}, the unstable region gradually approaches the flat-plate neutral boundary, and the $n$ curves recover towards the flat-plate reference. However, this recovery is local: the neutral curve and the local growth rate are properties of the base flow at a given position, whereas the perturbation amplitude in \ac{DNS} is the accumulated result of the entire upstream history. Two effects therefore delay the recovery of the amplitude. First, the cavity alters the receptivity through non-parallel effects, so the frequency content entering the downstream boundary layer is weighted differently from that of the flat plate and that of the local \ac{LSA}. Second, the problem is intrinsically advective, so this modified spectrum is transported downstream and amplified multiplicatively by the local growth rates, rather than being reset by them. The downstream evolution consequently does not necessarily need to follow the monotonic amplification of the flat-plate case, and even where the local stability boundaries collapse onto the flat-plate ones for $x\gg w$, the perturbation field from DNS does not immediately return to the flat-plate counterpart.

For wider gaps, the unstable region develops two distinct branches separated by a locally stable frequency range ($w\lesssim x\lesssim w+20$ in figure~\ref{fig:neutralcurvesB}). This locally stable region corresponds to the temporary and abrupt reduction in $n$ observed downstream of the wide gaps in figure~\ref{fig:nfactorCurves}. It also explains the slower recovery of the wider and deeper gaps shown in figure~\ref{fig:nfactorCurvesB} as the stable frequency range broadens with increasing gap width and depth.

After understanding the shape of the $n$-factor curves, the next step is to quantify the effect of a surface imperfection on transition. The metric $\Delta N$ was introduced by \citet{crouchKosoryginNg2006} and applied to gaps by \citet{jeffGaps}. This method is based on a change in the threshold value $N$ at which transition is declared, and it is constructed from the measured shift of the transition location as follows. The $n$-factor curve of the smooth reference surface, $n_\text{flat}(x)$, is computed once and used throughout (the gap never enters the stability calculation). The transition location $x_\text{flat}^\text{trans}$ is measured in the wind tunnel for the smooth surface, giving the reference threshold $N_0=n_\text{flat}(x_\text{flat}^\text{trans})$. For each gap configuration the transition location $x_\text{gap}^\text{trans}$ is then measured, and the threshold appropriate to that gap is the projected value onto the $n_\text{flat}$ curve, $N=n_\text{flat}(x_\text{gap}^\text{trans})$, so that $N=N_0-\Delta N$ with
\begin{equation}
	\Delta N := n_\text{flat}\big(x_\text{flat}^\text{trans}\big) - n_\text{flat}\big(x_\text{gap}^\text{trans}\big).
\end{equation}
Since a gap shifts the transition location upstream, $x_\text{gap}^\text{trans} < x_\text{flat}^\text{trans}$ and hence $\Delta N > 0$. In the present work the smooth reference is the Blasius boundary layer. The gap correlations of \citet{jeffGaps} against which the results below are compared were instead obtained for a boundary layer developing under an airfoil pressure distribution.

In the present numerical framework, the transition location cannot be measured because the governing equations are linear. A metric $\Delta n$ is therefore used to quantify how strongly the gap amplifies the disturbances arriving from upstream. $\Delta n$ is defined as an averaged difference between the $n$-factor curve for a given gap configuration and the flat-plate reference flow, i.e.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:deltan}
	\Delta n \equiv \Delta n_\text{gap}:=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\int_{\mathcal{X}} \left(n_\text{gap}(x)-n_\text{flat}(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{X}$ is a streamwise interval located downstream of the gap and $|\mathcal{X}|$ is its length. In the present study, $\mathcal{X}$ is taken to be $50$ spatial units long and to begin downstream of the initial amplitude-recovery zone, where the direct effects of the gap and of the associated non-parallel flow have decayed. Small dashed markers on top of each amplification curve in figure~\ref{fig:nfactorCurves} represent the streamwise locations in $\mathcal{X}$ used to compute the averaged $\Delta n$ values. Figure~\ref{fig:nfactorsketch} illustrates qualitatively the differences between the two metrics $\Delta N$ and $\Delta n$. Comparing the two therefore requires making some assumptions. In both, $n(x)$ is the envelope of the linear modes and $N$ is the threshold value at which transition is declared. The two methods are equivalent provided that (i) transition is well correlated with a linear threshold amplitude, (ii) $\Delta n$ captures the change in linear amplitude between the gap and the reference flow, and (iii) the measured $\Delta N$ corresponds to the same modes as those entering $\Delta n$. When these three conditions hold, raising the amplification curve by $\Delta n$ and lowering the threshold by $\Delta N$ place transition at the same station, so that the two approaches yield the same result, $\Delta n=\Delta N$. Conditions (i) and (iii) fail together once the gap ceases to act as a mere amplifier and generates disturbances of its own, and the remainder of the paper is concerned with where that happens.

The agreement between the contours of $\Delta n$ and the contours of $\Delta N$ is good. Both characteristic trends are reproduced: for narrow gaps ($w\lesssim30$), the isolines are predominantly vertical, where the amplification is primarily determined by the gap width; for shallow gaps ($d\lesssim1$), the isolines are predominantly horizontal, where the amplification is primarily determined by the gap depth. There is, however, a slight mismatch at higher $\Delta n$ values, where the numerical predictions overestimate the experimental $\Delta N$ results. There are several possible reasons for this difference.

Although the \ac{TS}-wave convective instability mechanism is two-dimensional, \citet{jeffGaps} conducted their experiments in a three-dimensional wind tunnel at low Mach number ($M<0.1$). In contrast, the present simulations use a two-dimensional domain and solve the incompressible Navier--Stokes equations. This difference in dimensionality may contribute to the observed discrepancies, while the incompressibility assumption may also play a role. The forcing model is a further source of difference. White noise excites all frequencies with equal energy, whereas in the wind tunnel free-stream turbulence, acoustic noise and surface roughness excite some frequencies more strongly than others. Since the gap amplifies each frequency by a different amount, the two measures may be dominated by different frequencies. In the simulations, $\Delta n$ is computed from the total amplitude $A(x)$, to which all forced frequencies contribute. In the experiment, $\Delta N$ is determined by the frequency that first reaches the transition threshold, and which frequency this is depends on the disturbance spectrum of the facility. Similarly, the disturbances are introduced here through a single localised source upstream of the gap, whereas in the experiment they enter the boundary layer along its whole length, including through the receptivity of free-stream disturbances at the gap edges, which the present model does not represent. In addition, the choice of the averaging interval $\mathcal{X}$ in~\eqref{eq:deltan} introduces a small, but non-negligible effect on the resulting $\Delta n$ values, particularly for gaps with large amplification, due to the streamwise variation of the $n$ curves. Despite these sources of discrepancy, the computed $\Delta n$ is in good qualitative agreement with the experimental $\Delta N$.% chktex 8

Finally, the effect of Reynolds number is investigated. To study the effect of Reynolds number on the amplification of upstream disturbances, it is necessary to understand first how the linear stability boundary changes with $\Rey$. The dependence of the neutral stability curve on Reynolds number is shown in figure~\ref{fig:2DdynamicsRe} for $\Rey\in[800,3000]$. Increasing $\Rey$ shifts the two-dimensional stability boundary towards smaller gap dimensions.

\subsection{Limit cycles (2D unstable regime)}\label{sec:bifurcations}
As the gap width or depth is increased the flow undergoes a supercritical Hopf bifurcation (solid green line in figure~\ref{fig:2Ddynamics1000}). The supercritical nature of the Hopf bifurcation is confirmed by the crossing of a single complex-conjugate pair of eigenvalues through the imaginary axis, while the remaining eigenvalues retain negative real parts. The expected normal-form scaling is further verified by tracking the amplitude of the emerging limit cycle along three continuation paths that cross the Hopf boundary in the $(w,d)$ plane, shown in the inset of figure~\ref{fig:hopfAmplitude}: $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1$ crosses it by varying the gap width at fixed depth, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_2$ along an oblique direction, and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_3$ by varying the depth at fixed width. Defining $\mu:=\pm\sqrt{(w/w_c-1)^2+(d/d_c-1)^2}$ as the relative distance from the critical point $(w_c,d_c)$ where each path crosses the Hopf boundary, positive on the unstable side, the normal form for a supercritical Hopf bifurcation then predicts
\begin{equation}
	A(\mu)\propto\sqrt{\mu},
\end{equation}
where $A$ is an appropriate measure of the limit cycle amplitude. Figure~\ref{fig:hopfAmplitude} shows the squared $L^2$ norm of $|\boldsymbol{u}'|$ over the whole domain $\Omega$ along $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_2$. In both cases $A^2$ depends approximately linearly on $\mu$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering
	\includegraphics{Images/hopfAmplitude.pdf}
	\caption{Amplitude evolution of the limit cycle for different crossing locations of the Hopf bifurcation in the $(w, d)$ parameter plane. The three continuation paths are indicated in the small plot on the right. The abscissa $\mu$ is a measure of the distance from the bifurcation point along each path, so that the bifurcation lies at $\mu=0$ on all three paths and $\mu>0$ on the unstable side. Paths $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_2$ (black and grey, left axis) are measured by the squared $L^2$ norm of $|\boldsymbol{u}'|$ over the whole domain $\Omega$. Open symbols mark the stable side of the bifurcation; closed symbols mark the unstable side, and the lines are linear fits on the unstable side.}\label{fig:hopfAmplitude}
\end{figure}
The remaining path, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_3$, crosses the Hopf boundary at fixed width $w=80$ by increasing the depth through $d\approx1.4$. A single complex-conjugate pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis here as well, yet the squared global norm does not grow linearly with $\mu$. This is not a failure of the normal form but a consequence of measuring the amplitude with a global norm. For gaps this wide the limit cycle frequency has fallen to the upper edge of the band of frequencies that become convectively unstable somewhere downstream of the gap (see figure~\ref{fig:freqUnstableMode}), so the oscillation at the downstream gap edge acts as a sustained harmonic source for the boundary layer behind it. The global $L^2$ norm then mixes two contributions: the oscillation localised at the gap edge, whose amplitude does scale as $\sqrt{\mu}$, and the downstream wave, whose amplitude is set by the integrated convective growth rate, and hence by the domain length and by how far below the upper branch of the local neutral stability curves the frequency lies, rather than by the distance from the bifurcation point. Restricting the norm to the region $\Omega'=[w-10,w]\times[-d/2,0.5]$, which encloses the end of the shear layer up to the downstream gap edge, isolates the first contribution. Measured in $\Omega'$, the squared amplitude along $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_3$ grows linearly with $\mu$ close to the bifurcation (figure~\ref{fig:hopfAmplitude}), as expected from the normal form.

The fundamental frequency $\omega$ of the limit cycles varies with Reynolds number and cavity geometry, as summarised in figure~\ref{fig:freqUnstableMode}. For narrow gaps ($w\lesssim60$), the frequencies form three distinct modal branches associated with the cavity geometry and recirculation dynamics. As the gap width increases at fixed depth, the dominant mode of the narrower cavity loses stability and a different periodic state emerges, with a frequency more representative of the larger cavity. The weaker interactions between branches observed across different depths indicate a secondary dependence on the gap geometry. For wide gaps ($w\gtrsim65$), the limit cycle frequencies approach the characteristic frequency range of convectively unstable \ac{TS} waves at the corresponding Reynolds number. The associated modes have longer streamwise scales and lower frequencies.

\section{Three-dimensional dynamics and transition}\label{sec:3Ddynamics}
Even though the primary convective instability of the boundary layer is two-dimensional, the global stability analysis of Section~\ref{sec:globalStability} has shown that a purely three-dimensional mode can become unstable before the two-dimensional one when the gap is sufficiently deep (figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000}). This section examines the nonlinear three-dimensional dynamics in order to identify the transition scenarios that a gap can produce. Three cases are distinguished, according to which instability is active. If only the three-dimensional mode is unstable, the perturbation saturates and the downstream boundary layer remains laminar. If the equilibrium has also lost stability to two-dimensional disturbances, as happens for deep gaps, the two instabilities interact and transition is triggered at the gap itself. If instead the two-dimensional instability sets in first, as happens for wide and shallow gaps, three-dimensionality develops only further downstream. The three cases are treated in turn below.

First, region II of figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000} is considered, where the purely three-dimensional mode has a positive growth rate while all two-dimensional modes are still stable. Nonlinear \ac{DNS} initialised along this mode shows the expected exponential growth, until the perturbation saturates at a finite amplitude and the flow settles onto a low-variance time-varying three-dimensional state without breaking down. Figure~\ref{fig:3dGapRegion2} shows where that saturated perturbation resides for a gap with width $w=24$ and depth $d=2$ at $\Rey=1000$ using the square root of a time- and spanwise-averaged energy of the perturbation velocity,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:3dperturbationEnergy}
	\eta:=\sqrt{{\langle|\boldsymbol{u}'|^2\rangle}_{\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{T}}}= {\left( \frac{1}{b}\int_{\mathcal{Z}} {\langle u'^2 + v'^2 + w'^2\rangle}_\mathcal{T}\, \mathrm{d}z\right)}^{1/2},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Z}=[0,b]$ is the spanwise domain and ${\langle \cdot \rangle}_\mathcal{T}$ denotes temporal expectation over the time interval $\mathcal{T}$. This quantity is a measure of the average magnitude of the three-dimensional perturbation velocity in a spanwise 2D slice of the domain.

Figure~\ref{fig:3dGapRegion2} shows where $\eta$ resides. It is concentrated inside the cavity and around the downstream edge, where it reaches magnitudes comparable to that of the base flow, and only a weak residual extends into the rest of the gap and the boundary layer behind the gap, where $\eta$ is small even by comparison with the base flow. Note that this distribution is qualitatively similar to that of the three-dimensional eigenmode shown in figure~\ref{fig:3Dmode}. Downstream of the gap the three-dimensional perturbation is therefore too weak to excite the boundary layer and provoke transition on its own. However, the three-dimensional structures inside the cavity act as a sustained low-amplitude source of low-frequency noise for the boundary layer behind it, and thereby provide a continuous excitation of the \ac{TS} waves downstream. These \ac{TS} waves amplify as they are convected downstream and become visible only near the downstream boundary of the domain (not shown for brevity).

The dynamics are qualitatively different once the equilibrium becomes unstable to two-dimensional disturbances. Widening the gap beyond the two-dimensional stability boundary produces a chaotic state that develops predominantly near the downstream edge of the gap (figure~\ref{fig:3dtransAtGap}). This behaviour corresponds to region V of figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000}.

Figure~\ref{fig:3dtransAtGap} illustrates the transition sequence for the deep-gap configuration $(w,d)=(19,4)$ at $\Rey=1000$. The flow is initialised with a small perturbation in the direction of the most unstable two-dimensional mode (figure~\ref{fig:3dtransAtGap_a}). It first evolves towards the corresponding periodic orbit, which is unstable to three-dimensional perturbations. As the instability develops, three-dimensional structures appear both within the cavity and downstream of the gap (figure~\ref{fig:3dtransAtGap_b}). The flow, then, subsequently departs from the two-dimensional periodic orbit and develops a chaotic state in the downstream boundary layer (figure~\ref{fig:3dtransAtGap_c}). At the later stages, the downstream flow exhibits increasingly complex three-dimensional structures leading to fully developed turbulence (figure~\ref{fig:3dtransAtGap_d}). Nevertheless, in the immediate vicinity of the downstream gap edge, the characteristic frequency of the underlying two-dimensional oscillation remains present. This behaviour is consistent with the strong non-\ac{TS}-wave spectral peak reported experimentally by \citet{jeffGaps}.

A qualitatively similar end state is reached from other initial conditions. Because the three-dimensional mode grows slightly faster than the two-dimensional one at this configuration in the linear regime, it is the first structure to emerge from a generic small disturbance, and the simulation has therefore also been initialised along the most unstable three-dimensional eigenmode. The disturbance then follows the predicted linear growth until nonlinear effects take over, at which point the flow leaves the eigenmode direction, the two-dimensional mode grows, and the flow again ends in a chaotic state similar to that of figure~\ref{fig:3dtransAtGap_d}. Which mode is excited first therefore changes the route slightly, but not the final state.

Both routes can be interpreted in terms of the triadic interactions produced by the quadratic nonlinearity of the Navier--Stokes equations, through which two Fourier components with spanwise wavenumbers and frequencies $(\beta_1,\omega_1)$ and $(\beta_2,\omega_2)$ force a third at $(\beta_1\pm\beta_2,\omega_1\pm\omega_2)$. Interacting with itself, the three-dimensional mode $(\pm\beta,\omega_{\mathrm{3D}})$ forces the harmonics $(2\beta,2\omega_{\mathrm{3D}})$ and $(2\beta,0)$, and the spanwise-uniform components $(0,0)$ and $(0,2\omega_{\mathrm{3D}})$. The latter components correspond to a distortion of the mean flow and a slow two-dimensional oscillation, respectively. Since $2\omega_{\mathrm{3D}}\approx0.05$ is far from $\omega_{\mathrm{2D}}\approx0.25$ and also because $\omega_{\mathrm{2D}}/\omega_{\mathrm{3D}}\approx 9.6$, this self-interaction cannot force the two-dimensional mode resonantly. The two-dimensional mode is instead linearly unstable on its own at this configuration and grows from the small spanwise-uniform content already present in the flow. Conversely, once the two-dimensional oscillation $(0,\omega_{\mathrm{2D}})$ is established, its interaction with the three-dimensional perturbation transfers energy to the sidebands $(\beta,\omega_{\mathrm{3D}}\pm k\omega_{\mathrm{2D}})$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$, so that the spanwise structure is now modulated at the frequency of the shear-layer oscillation and its harmonics. Repeated interactions among these components and the harmonics of both modes populate a broadband spectrum in both $\beta$ and $\omega$, which corresponds to the chaotic state observed downstream of the gap.

This result, together with the saturated region-II case discussed above, shows that the three-dimensional instability alone does not cause breakdown. On its own, the three-dimensional mode transfers energy only to its harmonics and to slow spanwise-uniform components, and it saturates. Transition at the gap requires both instabilities: the three-dimensional mode provides the spanwise structure, and the interaction of this structure with the high-frequency two-dimensional oscillation spreads energy across the spectrum and drives the flow to turbulence. To better understand this interaction, a Floquet analysis has been performed around the two-dimensional unstable limit cycle. The leading Floquet mode obtained in this way is shown in figure~\ref{fig:3dfloquetMode} for $(w,d)=(19,4)$ at $\Rey=1000$.

The third scenario occupies regions III and IV of figure~\ref{fig:3d2dDynamics1000}, in which the two-dimensional instability sets in before the three-dimensional one and the resulting limit cycle remains stable to three-dimensional perturbations. The subsequent evolution of the flow is therefore governed not by a secondary three-dimensional instability, but by where the frequency of the two-dimensional oscillation falls relative to the band of convectively unstable \ac{TS} waves, which for shallow gaps lies close to the upper edge of that band (figure~\ref{fig:freqUnstableMode}). This is what distinguishes the two regions. In region III the frequency lies above the upper branch, so the oscillation is damped as it is advected downstream and the boundary layer remains laminar. In region IV the frequency lies within the band, so the oscillation is amplified downstream of the gap and transition follows through the primary pathway of \ac{TS}-wave growth.

The passage from region III to region IV is gradual, in contrast to the abrupt crossings from region I to region II or from region II to region V. Figure~\ref{fig:L2normPO} illustrates the underlying mechanism. Deep within region III the oscillation is fully localised at the downstream edge of the gap and decays rapidly behind it. As the gap widens, the structure extends farther downstream and its decay becomes progressively slower, until it reaches far enough for the convectively unstable band to amplify it, at which point transition occurs.

For $w=60$ and $63$ the frequency lies above the upper branch at the gap, the excited wave is damped everywhere downstream, and the norm decays to negligible values within a few hundred spatial units. At $w=65$ the frequency lies inside the unstable band over part of the domain only: the norm grows to a maximum near $x-w\approx400$ and decays once the wave leaves that region. For $w\geq70$ the wave remains amplified over the whole domain and the norm grows monotonically to the end of it. In this latter case, the global and convective mechanisms reinforcing one another, leaving the downstream boundary layer fully destabilised.

\section{Summary and discussion}\label{sec:conclusions}
We have studied how a spanwise-uniform rectangular gap affects the stability and transition of a laminar incompressible boundary layer, using \ac{DNS} and global stability analysis over the range $w\in[0,130]$, $d\in[0,4]$ and $\Rey\in[800,3000]$. We have studied the gap from two complementary viewpoints. Where the equilibrium is stable, the gap has been treated as an amplifier of incoming disturbances and characterised by the amplification increment $\Delta n$. Beyond that regime, it has been treated as a dynamical system, and the bifurcations through which it loses stability have been followed. Three results stand out.

First, the gap equilibrium loses stability through a supercritical Hopf bifurcation, and the resulting neutral curve is not monotonic in width: increasing the width, the critical depth first decreases, reaching a minimum where the two gap edges interfere most strongly, and then increases towards the isolated-step limit. Increasing the Reynolds number moves the whole boundary towards smaller gaps.

Second, while the equilibrium remains stable the gap acts only as an amplifier to perturbations that reach it from upstream, and this effect is fully captured by the increment $\Delta n$ obtained from stochastically forced linearised simulations. The resulting $\Delta n$ contour map shows good agreement with the $\Delta N$ experimental values measured by \citet{jeffGaps}. Local \ac{LSA} has proven opposite Reynolds-number trends followed by narrow and wide gaps. Increasing $\Rey$ narrows the band of convectively unstable frequencies but strengthens the shear layer, and the width of the gap decides which of the two prevails. In a narrow gap the higher growth rates act over too short a distance to overcome the loss of bandwidth, so that $\Delta n$ decreases with $\Rey$. In a wide gap the higher growth rates act over a distance long enough to do so, and $\Delta n$ increases with $\Rey$.

Third, transition at the gap is not set by the linear instability of the steady flow. The purely three-dimensional mode of the equilibrium saturates and leaves the downstream boundary layer laminar; breakdown instead requires a two-dimensional limit cycle that is itself unstable to three-dimensional perturbations. This condition is both necessary and sufficient, and the upper envelope of the two- and three-dimensional neutral curves reproduces the experimental bypass boundary of \citet{jeffGaps} over the whole range considered.

These results separate two roles that a gap can play, and the distinction determines how transition should be predicted. As long as the flow over the gap remains steady, the gap merely modifies the amplification of a convective instability that it does not create; its whole effect is then contained in the single increment $\Delta n$, and the variable-$N$ method \citep{crouchKosoryginNg2006,crouch2021predicting} applies. Once the gap sustains a limit cycle that is unstable to three-dimensional perturbations, it generates the disturbances that break the flow down itself. Transition, then, no longer proceeds through the amplification of incoming waves, and no critical $N$-factor can describe it. What separates the two regimes is a bifurcation of the flow, which can be computed directly and without calibration, and which moves towards smaller gaps as the Reynolds number increases, so that tolerances set at one flight condition do not carry over to another.
